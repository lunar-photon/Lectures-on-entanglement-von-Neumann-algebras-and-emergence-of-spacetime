\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}
\markboth{Preface}{Preface}

Quantum mechanics is usually taught with the Hilbert space as the starting point: a system has a space of
state vectors, observables are operators acting on it, and a composite system is described by a tensor
product. This picture works perfectly for a spin, an atom, or any system with finitely many degrees of
freedom. It stops working for a quantum field cut into two regions, for a many-body system in the
thermodynamic limit, and for quantum gravity in the limit where a classical spacetime appears. In each of these
cases the entanglement between a subsystem and its surroundings is infinite, and the tensor-product split that
defines ``subsystem'' in the first place no longer exists.

These notes develop the replacement. A subsystem is described by the algebra of operators an observer can
measure, not by a factor of the Hilbert space. The classification of such algebras into types I, II and III
turns out to be a classification of how a subsystem is entangled with the rest of the world, and the modular
theory of Tomita and Takesaki supplies a notion of time and temperature for any such algebra. In the second
half of the notes these tools are applied to holography, where they show that regions of a gravitational
spacetime, their causal structure, their horizons and their entropy can all be read off from the algebras of
a boundary quantum system.

\section*{Who these notes are for}

The reader is assumed to know linear algebra (vectors, matrices, eigenvalues) and undergraduate quantum
mechanics at the level of Griffiths or Sakurai: kets and bras, operators, the Schr\"odinger and Heisenberg
pictures, spin, the harmonic oscillator, and density matrices. Some familiarity with statistical mechanics
(Gibbs states and partition functions) helps in Chapters 3--5. Everything beyond that is built on the page
before it is used.

\section*{How the notes are organised}

Chapters 1--5 develop the mathematics using only finite quantum systems and free fields: why the tensor
product fails (Chapter~1), von Neumann algebras and the GNS construction (Chapter~2), algebras with a trace
(Chapter~3), type III algebras and modular theory (Chapter~4), and the crossed product that turns a type III
algebra into one with a well-defined entropy (Chapter~5). Chapters 6--9 apply these tools to the AdS/CFT
correspondence and to simple models of quantum gravity, and Chapter~10 summarises.

Three kinds of boxes appear throughout. \emph{Physics Connection} boxes compute the same quantity twice ---
once the way you already know from ordinary quantum mechanics, and once with the new construction --- and show
that the answers agree. \emph{Worked Example} boxes carry out a construction on a small system with explicit
numbers. \emph{Key Result} boxes collect theorems and their proofs.

Calculations are written as chains of equalities in which every non-trivial step carries a small number over
its equals sign, for example $A\eqstep{1}B\eqstep{2}C$. The sentence immediately after the chain explains each
numbered step, so no step is left for the reader to reconstruct. Where a result is quoted from the research
literature rather than derived, the text says so.

\section*{Sources}

The logical development follows the lectures of H.~Liu~\cite{Liu2025}, which review this subject at research
level. The treatment of entanglement in quantum field theory draws on Witten~\cite{Witten2018}; the
mathematical foundations of algebraic quantum mechanics on Strocchi~\cite{Strocchi} and Haag~\cite{HaagBook};
the statistical interpretation of states on Slavnov~\cite{Slavnov2001}; and the crossed-product treatment of
de~Sitter entropy on Chandrasekaran, Longo, Penington and Witten~\cite{CLPW}. Numerical values quoted in worked
examples were checked by direct computation.
